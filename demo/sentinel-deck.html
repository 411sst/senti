Sentinel

Warning ICU teams before a patient deteriorates, with eight small models that work together.

October 2026 · MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD

Setup

One question, every hour

What counts as deterioration

Invasive ventilation starts, a vasopressor starts, or the patient dies, whichever comes first.

The data

MIMIC-IV v3.11: 56,157 ICU stays and 2.46 million patient-hours from one Boston hospital.

Why we start at hour 6

73% of events happen in the first 6 hours. Those patients arrive already deteriorating, so there is nothing to predict early.

Setup

Keeping the test fair

Every split is by patient. The first validation half calibrates the risk scores, so that "10% risk" means about 10% of those hours are followed by an event. The second half picks the alarm threshold, so it is never tuned on the data the calibration was fitted to. Test patients are scored once, at the end.

Patient-hour
One patient for one hour. A 3-day stay is 72 patient-hours. The test set has 361,229 of them from 8,375 stays; 4.74% are followed by an event within 24 hours.
Alarm budget
The share of patient-hours on which a model may alarm. At 2.6%, a full 20-bed ICU gets about 12 to 13 alarm-hours a day. Every model gets the same budget, so none can look better by alarming more.
AUPRC area under the precision-recall curve
How well a model ranks the hours before an event above all other hours. Random guessing scores 0.047 here; perfect is 1.0. Our main metric, because events are rare.
ECE expected calibration error
The average gap between predicted risk and what happened. If 1,000 hours are each given 10% risk, about 100 should be followed by an event. 0 is perfect.
How it works

How it fits together

  • Each agent reads one kind of data and sends a 32-number summary (its latent vector) and a risk every hour.
  • Both heads read the same 208 numbers, but are trained on different targets.
  • Noisy-OR combines their probabilities into the chance that either event happens.
  • NEWS22 is computed exactly to the published bands. Risks are calibrated with isotonic regression3.
  • The alarm threshold is fixed on validation data and never changed at test time.
  • Dashed grey is the explanation path. It sends nothing back, so removing the LLM leaves every score unchanged.

One contract for every agent

An agent reads one kind of data, recorded before the current hour, and is trained on its own.

Every hour it outputs a 32-number latent vector and a calibrated risk. If its data is missing, it outputs a learned "no data" value.

Because every agent has the same output, one can be added or removed and only the two fusion heads need retraining, which takes minutes.

How it works

Why these models

A TCN for vitals

Vitals are the only input that arrives as a dense time series. A temporal convolutional network (TCN) reads a window of past hours using convolutions that only look backwards, so it cannot see the future4.

Bai et al. found TCNs matched or beat LSTMs and GRUs on standard sequence tasks, while training in parallel with a fixed, known look-back5. TCNs have been used on ICU data for sepsis6 and acute critical illness7, and YAIB benchmarks one on MIMIC-IV8.

Small MLPs for the rest

Labs, ECG, fluid, resp and cadence already come as summary numbers per hour: the latest value, how old it is, counts over a window.

A two-layer MLP turns those into the 32-number latent. Their change over time is handled once, inside the fusion heads.

Why a GBM alone isn't enough

Gradient-boosted trees are the strongest single model type on tabular ICU data8,9, which is why one sits inside Sentinel as an agent10. On its own it falls short:

  1. It scores each hour separately. Trends must be hand-built as features; the fusion heads read the last 29 hours of every agent.
  2. It uses the same splits for every patient. The fusion re-weights the agents for each patient, every hour.
  3. Given the same data, it ranks worse: AUPRC 0.287 vs 0.309 (10 of 10 seeds). For death alone, 0.189 vs 0.340.
How it works

Inside a head

Attention

Each agent's vector is scored on its own, and a softmax turns the 8 scores into weights that add up to 1 (additive attention11). Agents are never compared with each other, so it is not a Transformer. The weights change every hour, for every patient.

29 hours of memory

Each TCN block adds 2 × 2 × dilation hours of reach: 4 + 8 + 16 = 28, plus the current hour. The number follows from the dilations; we did not tune it.

Two heads

Same design, different targets: treatment start, or death. One head trained on both had to compromise. Alone, the Mortality head ranks death at AUPRC 0.340, 1.8× the GBM's 0.189.

How it works

Numbers beat sentences

Most multi-agent LLM systems have agents talk to each other in sentences. We tested that against passing the latent vectors directly, with everything else held fixed.

AUPRC on identical rows

Same three agents (vitals, labs, NEWS2), same 150 stays (6,185 patient-hours, 4.41% positive). Both arms calibrated on the same validation data.

In the text arm, each agent's output becomes a templated sentence, and a local Llama-3.1-8B reads the last 4 hours of sentences and replies with a risk from 0 to 100. Text loses most on death (AUPRC 0.025 vs 0.193), and calibrating its scores does not improve its ranking.

Results

How it ranks

Test AUPRC on held-out MIMIC · 361,229 patient-hours · 4.74% positive

"Published spec" models are our rebuilds of each paper's feature recipe, trained on our cohort. They are not the commercial products.

The comparators: an eCART-style GBM (the recipe behind an FDA-cleared score)12, YAIB LightGBM (the community MIMIC benchmark)8, and Sadanandan's XGBoost, the only published model with our exact label13.

Against eCART-style: +0.029 AUPRC [+0.025, +0.032], ahead in 10 of 10 seeds. Against our own GBM, built from the same data as Sentinel: ahead in 10 of 10 seeds. Both published recipes score within 0.007 of our GBM.

Why a 2.6% budget?

We wanted every model held to one budget near 2% of hours. NEWS2 is a whole-number score, so it can't hit 2% exactly. Its closest cut-off here is a score of 11 or more, which fires on 2.62% of hours.

We used that as the budget and set each model's threshold on validation data to fire on the same share. In a full 20-bed ICU, that is about 12 to 13 alarm-hours a day.

The ward trigger, NEWS2 ≥ 5, doesn't work here: ICU patients meet it on 45% of hours.

From 0.5% to 10% of patient-hours, Sentinel stays 7 to 13 points ahead. YAIB sits just below eCART-style at every budget (hover for its numbers).

The cost: more patients see at least one alarm. 11.8% of stays that never deteriorate get a Sentinel alarm, vs 6.8% and 6.2%. Total alarm-hours are the same; Sentinel's alarms are shorter and spread over more patients (4.0 alarm-hours per quiet stay alarmed, vs 5.6 and 5.7).

In practice

Scoring in real time

Hourly, by default

MIMIC stores every vital sign with the time it was taken. Our main pipeline groups readings into hourly slots, averages each slot ("binning"), and scores once an hour. A reading taken at 10:05 waits for the 11:00 score.

On arrival, as a test

We rebuilt the pipeline to keep every reading and re-score as each one arrives. Grouping the raw readings back into hours reproduces all 2,460,983 hourly rows exactly. Each reading has two times: when it was taken, and when it reached the hospital record (a median of 29 minutes later, after a nurse checks it).

+30 minearlier first alarm on average (95% CI 12 to 55). The median gain is 0: most first alarms don't move. 28% more alarm starts.
80%of first alarms that still come late are waiting on an hourly input: labs, ECG, GBM, cadence, fluid or resp.
0.314 → 0.292AUPRC when vitals come through the hospital record, with its delay, instead of straight from the monitor.
4.5%of vital-sign hours in MIMIC hold more than one reading, so there is little new data inside an hour.

The gain would grow with minute-level monitor data, and with the six hourly inputs streamed too. MIMIC has no minute-level data, so this is as far as we can test it. One seed so far.

In practice

Explaining an alarm

When an alarm fires, Sentinel builds a short summary: each agent's attention weight and risk, the 7 NEWS2 sub-scores, and how 5 vitals moved over the last 4 hours. A local LLM (gemma4:12b, on the same machine) turns it into one sentence. It is never given the risk score, and its output never goes back into the model.
